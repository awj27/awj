Most teams I meet are running MEDDPICC as a form. Eight fields in the CRM, filled in after the meeting, scored by the rep, admired by the manager, and about as useful for forecasting as a horoscope.

I wrote the book on it. I did not write it so you could fill in a form.

MEDDPICC is a set of questions. Each letter is something you need to know about a deal before you can say you understand it. Ask the questions before the meeting and they tell you what to go and find out. Ask them afterwards and they tell you what you missed. The framework only does its job when you are honest about the gaps.

## What the letters mean

MEDDIC is the original. Dick Dunkel and Jack Napoli built it at PTC. Six letters, each one a thing you must know about a deal.

Metrics. The quantifiable value the customer gets from you, in numbers they would defend in their own boardroom and in their words rather than yours.

Economic Buyer. The person with discretionary control of the budget. The one who can say yes when everyone else has said no, and no when everyone else has said yes.

Decision Criteria. The formal and informal standards the customer will use to choose. Yours, if you got there first. Your competitor's, if they did.

Decision Process. The steps, the people and the timing between a decision being made and a contract being signed.

Identify Pain, or as I prefer it, Implicate the Pain. Finding the pain is the easy bit. The work is tying that pain to its cost, and your solution to its relief, until the customer can feel what doing nothing costs them.

Champion. A person with power and influence who sells for you when you are not in the room. A Champion wants you to win because your winning helps them win.

MEDDICC adds a second C for Competition. Anyone or anything competing for the same budget, and that includes doing nothing. The competitor I have lost to most often in twenty years never had a sales team.

MEDDPICC adds a P for Paper Process. Legal, procurement, security. The part of the deal that slides a forecast from this quarter into next in the final week, because nobody asked in month one who signs, who reviews, and how long their queue is.

## MEDDIC, MEDDICC or MEDDPICC?

Use the longest one. The thinking is identical across all three. The extra letters stop you pretending that Competition and Paper Process are somebody else's problem. Nobody ever lost a deal because they understood too much of it.

One letter I would not add is a third C for Compelling Event. If you need an extra letter for urgency, you have missed the point of three you already have. Pain plus Metrics plus a Champion is urgency. A compelling event is what you get when those three are real. The end of your quarter is not one, and the customer does not care about it.

## Checklist or questions?

Here is how the checklist version goes wrong. A deal review, any company, any quarter.

**Manager:** "Who is the Economic Buyer?"

**Rep:** "Eve. The CFO."

**Manager:** "Have we met her?"

**Rep:** "Charlie says she is fully on board."

**Manager:** "Right. Green it is."

What that team has is a Champion's opinion of an Economic Buyer. What they have written down is an Economic Buyer. The CRM cannot tell the difference, and by the time the forecast call comes round, neither can the manager.

The questions version runs the other way. Before the next meeting with Charlie, the rep writes down what she knows about each letter and how she knows it. Economic Buyer: named, never met, status second-hand.

That is now the agenda. The meeting exists to get Charlie to put her in front of Eve, or to find out why he will not. The framework has told the rep what to do on Tuesday. That is what it is for.

A checklist measures how confident the rep feels. Questions measure how much evidence the deal has. Only one of those forecasts anything.

## Inspect the deal, not the rep

Deal reviews turn into box-ticking because they turn into interrogations. The rep is on trial, so the rep defends. Worked, chased, pushed, nurtured. Effort verbs. None of them are MEDDPICC.

Put the deal on trial instead. Four questions, in order:

1. Do we belong in this deal?
2. Where is the deal strong for us?
3. Where is it weak, and for what reasons is it weak?
4. What actions move it forward?

Run it that way and the rep stops defending and starts thinking, because the review is now helping her win. The leader learns something true about the forecast. If you cannot articulate the Champion's economic case in three sentences, the deal is not real, and no amount of effort makes it real.

## Nobody regrets qualifying out

The hardest part of MEDDPICC is what you do when the letters come back empty. I coined "Nobody regrets qualifying out" to give sellers permission to walk, and I have never seen it proved wrong. One of two things happens. Either you were right, and the opportunity was never worth the time, or the customer's behaviour corrects and they start treating the engagement with the respect a real deal deserves.

Twenty years in sales taught me that the deals which hurt were never the ones I qualified out of. They were the ones I kept in the forecast because the form looked full.
