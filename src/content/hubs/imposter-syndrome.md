Only the self-aware suffer from imposter syndrome.

I first wrote that line down as [Maxim 10](/journal/imposter-syndrome-self-awareness/), and I have put it to a lot of people since. Every one of them agreed. But the longer I sit with it, the more I think it looks at the wrong end of the problem. We talk endlessly about the syndrome. We never talk about the imposters.

## Who are the imposters?

Not you. The actual imposters. The ones overselling themselves, claiming success they did not authentically achieve, bigging themselves up beyond their worth. The LinkedIn headline that reads "I took the company from $x to $xxx" when there were a hundred other people in the building. Without them there would be no imposter syndrome, because there would be nothing to be mistaken for.

Think about what the feeling actually is. You walk into the room and you worry about what people are thinking. Who does he think he is? He should not be in here. Clearly, he is out of his depth.

You only imagine those sentences because you know they are what people think about actual imposters. You have seen it. You have thought it yourself, about someone else.

So by virtue of having imposter syndrome, you are not an imposter. It would not be a syndrome if you were. The real ones never feel it. That is rather the point of them.

## When does it show up?

Only when you are growing. A self-aware person does not get imposter syndrome doing something they have done a hundred times. It arrives at the edge, in the uncharted territory of your own potential, where you cannot yet prove to yourself that you belong. That uncertainty is the feeling. It is also the only evidence you get, in the moment, that you are moving.

A-Players are uncomfortable being comfortable. When I wrote that maxim, imposter syndrome was on the list of discomforts, next to the hunger and the nagging question of whether you are doing enough. Roger Federer hired a new coach at 33, with 17 Grand Slams already won, and chose to be bad at a new backhand in public. You do not do that without feeling, on some Tuesday in practice, like a fraud. He won three more majors after 35.

And that is okay. The two conditions are that you are kind to yourself while it happens, and that you do not let it hold you back. Feel it, name it, walk in anyway.

## The first deal, the first team, the first stage

Sales is where I learnt this. Early in my career I felt like I was playing sales on hard mode while everyone around me was on easy mode. The first time I sat across from a real Economic Buyer, someone who could say yes or no to the whole deal, every one of those borrowed sentences ran through my head.

What I know now is that the rep who feels nothing in that room is the one to worry about. The feeling means you understand what is at stake. MEDDIC gave me a way to turn the uncertainty into questions I could go and answer, and the B-Player version of me became the A-Player version. The feeling did not go away. It got a job.

Then the first team. I was a store manager at Phones 4u in 2004 and Head of Sales at Everest Home Improvements before the year was out. Nobody hands you a manual. You are certain the team will work out that you are making it up as you go, and the honest answer is that every first-time leader is.

My biggest failure as a leader came later and had nothing to do with making it up. I avoided a hard conversation with a failing executive for the best part of a year, telling myself I was gathering context, when the truth is I did not yet have the guts or the tools. The feeling had been telling me to go and get the tools. Eventually I listened and hired a coach.

Then the first stage. Mine was at a holiday resort in 2001, as an entertainer and DJ, so I had a head start, and it still did nothing for me the first time I walked out in front of a sales kickoff as the person flown in to tell them how to sell. Founding a company was the same feeling at a larger scale. The first hire. The first time someone introduced me as the CEO and I glanced over my shoulder to see who they meant.

Every one of those firsts had the same signature. New territory, no proof yet, and a self-aware mind filling the silence with other people's imagined verdicts. Every one of them was growth.

## Why it is a skill

Your imposter syndrome is a skill. A strength. An indicator of your emotional intelligence. It means you can read a room, see yourself the way others might, and care whether your claims are earned. The actual imposters cannot do any of that, which is exactly why they are so comfortable.

Authenticity is felt, not found. People cannot always say why they trust someone, but they feel the difference between earned and performed, the way the brain lights up for a Rembrandt it believes is real. In an ever-inauthentic world, where unearned egos go unchecked, your authenticity becomes your number one intangible asset.

One honest caveat, because I wrote a Maxim about it. We fight most what we almost are. The self-congratulatory headline winds me up precisely because I know how the pull works, and so do you.

You feel the pull too. The imposter gives in to it. You check your claims against what you actually did before you make them, and that habit is the whole difference. Authenticity beats deception, in a deal and in a career, because the room finds out either way.

The imposters are busy performing. You are busy growing. Over twenty years, only one of those compounds.

So the next time it shows up, do not go looking for a cure. Write down what you are doing that is new. That list is the growth. The feeling is the receipt.

What are you growing into right now?
